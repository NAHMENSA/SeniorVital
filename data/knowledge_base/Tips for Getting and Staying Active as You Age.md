Tips for Getting and Staying Active as You Age. Deciding to be
physically active at any age is a positive choice for your well-being
and has many important health benefits. As part of an active lifestyle,
older adults should include a combination of exercise types. Even small
amounts of activity throughout the day count toward the recommended 150
minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity and two days of
muscle-strengthening activities each week. Starting and maintaining a
change in routine can be difficult. There are strategies to help
increase physical activity levels. Increase activity gradually over
weeks or months. Begin with lower-intensity activities suitable for your
current fitness level. Then increase frequency, intensity, and duration
over time. Gradual progression builds confidence, motivation, and a
regular routine. If there is a break in routine due to illness or
travel, return to activity slowly. Older adults without pre-existing
injury or chronic condition who plan to gradually increase activity
generally do not need to consult a doctor before starting. However, for
people with or without chronic conditions, doctors and exercise
professionals can assess current activity levels, offer advice on
suitable activities and ways to progress safely, and provide ongoing
support through follow-up appointments. Warm up before exercise and cool
down after to help prevent injury and other negative health events. A
warm-up prepares muscles and allows heart rate and breathing to increase
gradually. A cool-down allows a gradual decrease at the end. Talk with
your doctor about exercising with chronic conditions and how existing
health issues may affect your ability to be regularly active. Be as
physically active as your abilities and conditions allow. Set specific
and realistic goals for physical activity. Goals are more useful when
specific and measurable, for example, walking 10 minutes three times per
week. Review goals regularly as you progress. Track your activity daily
using a device (like a pedometer) or a written log. Some people share
their activity with a friend, doctor, or exercise professional for
accountability and feedback. Celebrate successes. Identify barriers to
physical activity. Common barriers include lack of access to safe
places, cost of equipment, concerns about ability or injury, time,
weather, and interests. Problem-solve with a doctor or exercise
professional to find specific solutions. Re-evaluate barriers over time
as situations change. Examples of solutions: for bad weather or poor air
quality, walk indoors at a mall or supermarket, or use online fitness
classes. For cost and expensive equipment, try free online videos, use
household items like books or detergent jugs for muscle-strengthening,
or add activity through chores, gardening, dancing, or playing with
grandchildren. For fatigue or low energy, plan activity for times when
you feel most energetic. For lack of motivation, enjoyment, or
confidence, consider working with a personal trainer, find social
support by exercising with a friend, or join a virtual community. Group
physical activities such as exercise classes, walking groups, or
exercise buddy systems provide movement and also offer social
engagement, friendship, and emotional support. Social support from
friends and family increases motivation and is key to success.
Participate in a variety of activities. Older adults should include a
weekly combination of aerobic, muscle-strengthening, and balance
activities. Variety makes activity more enjoyable and reduces overuse
injury risk. Activity can include exercise, chores, errands, walking or
biking for transportation, or leisure-time physical activity. Examples:
playing pickleball with friends, climbing stairs, yard work, swimming,
dancing, carrying groceries, helping care for grandchildren, or taking
an exercise class. Try both indoor and outdoor activities. Find multiple
ways to incorporate enjoyable activities into your life. Ensure shoes
fit well and provide adequate foot support with non-slip soles, enough
toe space, and good heel and arch support. Replace shoes when they wear
out. Wear clothing that allows easy movement and does not hinder
activity. Use fabrics that wick sweat away from the skin and dry
quickly; avoid cotton and look for terms like "moisture-wicking".
